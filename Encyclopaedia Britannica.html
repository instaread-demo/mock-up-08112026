Dark matter is matter whose presence astronomers detect through its gravitational pull rather than any light it gives off. Observations indicate that it accounts for roughly a quarter of the universe's total matter-energy content — far more than the visible stars and gas we can actually see.

The idea dates to the 1930s, when measurements of galaxy clusters suggested that visible material alone could not supply enough gravity to hold them together. Decades later, studies of how stars orbit within individual galaxies pointed to the same conclusion: the outer stars move far too quickly for the visible mass alone, so a large halo of unseen material must surround each galaxy.

Modern evidence comes from gravitational lensing — massive structures bending the light of objects behind them — and from X-ray observations of colliding galaxy clusters, where the hot visible gas lags behind while the bulk of the mass passes through undisturbed. Both point to the same invisible component dominating the total mass.

What dark matter is made of remains an open question. Leading candidates are heavy, slow-moving particles that interact only weakly with ordinary matter, along with lighter hypothetical particles such as axions. Laboratory detectors deep underground and experiments at particle accelerators continue the search, so far without a confirmed detection.
