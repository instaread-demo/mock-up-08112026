Giving While Living: The Philosophy Reshaping Big Philanthropy

A century-old idea is enjoying a second life in billionaire circles: that a vast fortune is not a trophy to be kept but a responsibility to be discharged — preferably while its owner is still alive to steer the results.

Advocates of the "giving while living" school argue that wealth held idle until death represents a missed opportunity. Money deployed early, they say, can compound its social impact the same way capital compounds returns, and the donor gets to see what works and correct what doesn't.

Caption: the modern pledge movement traces its roots to 19th-century industrialist philanthropy.

Critics counter that lifetime giving concentrates enormous influence over public priorities in a handful of private hands. Foundations answer to no electorate, and a donor's pet cause is not always society's most urgent need.

What both camps agree on is that the stakes are rising. With the largest generational wealth transfer in history now under way, the question of when — and how — fortunes should be given back has moved from the seminar room to the boardroom.
